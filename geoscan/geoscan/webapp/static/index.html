How OreSight works

        
OreSight ranks ground by how likely it is to host copper, using only satellite data.
          It finds the surface signs that copper-bearing fluids leave behind and the structures those fluids used.
          It does not see copper itself. Every result is a surface-evidence ranking to guide field checks,
          geophysics and drilling.


        
1. The four alteration minerals

        
Hot fluids that carry copper change the rocks they pass through. Four signs are mapped:

        

          
LayerWhat it isSatellite and signal

          
Muscovite / sericiteWhite mica (phyllic alteration), usually closest to the oreEMIT, Al-OH absorption near 2200 nm

          
Chlorite / epidoteGreen minerals (propylitic alteration), usually an outer haloEMIT, Fe-OH (2250 nm) and Mg-OH (2330 nm) absorptions

          
Gossan / iron oxideRusty iron staining, the weathered cap of sulphidesEMIT, Fe³⁺ absorption near 900 nm

          
Quartz / silicificationSilica added by the fluidsASTER thermal emissivity (90 m)

        

        
For each EMIT pixel the depth of the absorption and its uncertainty are measured on every usable date.
          A pixel counts as a detection only when the absorption is clearly larger than its uncertainty.
          Detections are then spread into smooth zones (a sharp core plus a wider halo) and scaled so that
          100 % = the strongest 2 % of this area. Percentages are relative within one area.


        
2. Structures (lineaments)

        
Fluids rise along faults, fractures and shear zones. Straight features are traced from an elevation
          model: hillshades from 8 sun directions plus valley and ridge axes. Lines shorter than 800 m are dropped.
          Cliff and plateau edges (scarps) are kept on the map but not used for scoring.
          Lineaments come from terrain, so buried structures under flat ground can be missed. Aeromagnetic data is
          the standard tool for those.


        
3. The Copper Prospectivity Index (CPI)

        
H = 0.30·muscovite + 0.28·quartz + 0.22·gossan + 0.20·chlorite

          CPI = 0.70·H + 0.30·exp(−distance to lineament / 250 m)

        
These are the default weights. All of them can be changed under Adjust parameters.
          CPI is shown 0-100 %, with contours at 28 % (Tier 3), 50 % (Tier 2) and 70 % (Tier 1).


        
4. Prospective zones and field-check locations

        
The result of this method is a region, not a point. The satellite pixels are 60-90 m, each detection is
          spread over a few hundred metres, and the data describe the surface while the ore is underground. So the method
          can say "the mineralisation is probably somewhere in this zone", but it cannot say "drill exactly here".

        
Prospective zones are the main result: connected ground with CPI at or above the Tier 2 threshold (50 %).
          A zone is Tier 1 if it reaches 70 % somewhere, otherwise Tier 2. Separate weak anomalies that only reach 28 % are
          listed as Tier 3 zones. The 28 % contour around a zone is its outer halo. Zones are ranked by tier, then by peak CPI.

        
Suggested field-check locations are starting points for field work inside the zones: the highest-CPI
          cells, kept at least 250 m apart. Their exact position is not significant, and they are not drill collars.
          Follow-up is staged: Tier 1 → detailed mapping, systematic sampling and ground geophysics; Tier 2 → reconnaissance
          mapping and sampling; Tier 3 → check during regional traverses. Drilling decisions come only after that work.

        
Validation supports this: at Thanewasna the Tier 1 zone covers 17.5 % of the block and holds 53 % of the known
          lode area and 6 of the 8 boreholes that intersect a lode, while only 3 of the top 10 point locations lie within
          100 m of a lode.


        
5. Reading the maps

        
    
          
  • Hatched grey areas = "Not assessed". EMIT had no usable view of the ground there. This does
            not mean there is no alteration. In those areas the CPI uses quartz and structure only.
    • 
          
  • Contour lines mark the zone boundaries shown in each map's legend.
    • 
          
  • Click a zone or a field-check location on the map for its details. Click a row in Zones to jump to it.
    • 
          
  • Reference geochemistry (stream sediment samples) is shown as squares, coloured and sized by the element
            you choose. Each sample reflects the ground upstream of it, so no surface is drawn between samples, and high
            values can simply reflect the rock type (basalt is naturally rich in copper). It is not used in the CPI.
            Where an area has no usable samples, the option says so.
    • 
          
  • Reference geophysics (magnetic anomaly, Bouguer gravity) is listed under the map layers where ground
            stations exist for the area. Diamonds are the measured stations; the coloured surface and contours are
            interpolated between them. These layers are for viewing and download only: they are not used in the CPI
            or the zone ranking. The stations are typically about 1 km apart, so they show the regional pattern
            and cannot confirm or reject a single zone.
    •

8. What the validation numbers mean

        
AUC is the chance that a known mineralised spot scores higher than a random non-mineralised one.
          0.5 = no better than chance, 1.0 = perfect. At Thanewasna the CPI is tested against 30 mapped Cu lodes
          and 47 boreholes. Kehalpur has no lode or borehole coordinates, so its checks are consistency tests against
          NGDR explored blocks and the GSI geology map.


        
9. Known limits

        
    
          
  • The method predicts regions, not exact points. Use the zones to decide where to work, and treat the
            point locations only as places to start.
    • 
          
  • An Al-OH absorption at 2190-2215 nm is read as white mica, but kaolinite and smectite absorb at similar positions.
    • 
          
  • Iron oxide from satellites cannot separate a true gossan from laterite or ferruginous soil.
    • 
          
  • The weights follow porphyry-style zoning. The deposit type (for example VMS at Kehalpur) may call for different weights.
    • 
          
  • The quartz layer is coarse (90 m) and the silica ratio SI has no verified primary source.
